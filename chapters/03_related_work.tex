\chapter{Related Work}\label{chapter:related-work}

\section{Combining ACID and BASE}\label{sec:rw-acid-base}

\subsection{Static classification of transactions}\label{sec:rw-static}

Salt~\cite{xie2014salt} is the closest neighbour of this work. It lets a
developer convert the few performance-critical ACID transactions of an
application into BASE transactions, split into smaller steps, while the
remaining ACID transactions keep their guarantees through an additional
isolation level. The mixture of the two semantics is therefore explicit
and intended, as it is here.

Two differences separate it from this thesis. Salt decides \emph{which}
transactions are BASE, statically, when the application is written,
whereas this thesis decides \emph{when} to use each mode, at runtime, from
measured system state. And Salt targets single-schema replicated storage,
whereas the setting here is a multi-model system in which inconsistency is
produced by automatic propagation between models.

RedBlue consistency~\cite{li2012redblue} makes a similar classification at
a finer granularity: operations labelled blue execute without coordination
and operations labelled red require it, with the labelling fixed at design
time and constrained by commutativity requirements. The same contrast
applies, one level down.

\subsection{Runtime-adaptive consistency}\label{sec:rw-adaptive}

Consistency Rationing~\cite{kraska2009rationing} is the closest
methodological precedent. Data is divided into categories that receive
different guarantees, and the middle category switches its consistency
level at runtime according to a policy driven by monitored statistics and
expected cost. The mode oracle of this thesis is the same idea applied to
a different signal: the measured dirty fraction rather than an economic
estimate.

Harmony~\cite{chihoub2012harmony} adapts the number of replicas consulted
per read in a cloud storage system based on an estimate of the probability
of returning stale data, which is again a runtime decision driven by a
monitored proxy.

\subsection{Bounded inconsistency}\label{sec:rw-bounded}

Yu and Vahdat~\cite{yu2000conit} treat consistency as a continuous
quantity and allow applications to bound the divergence they tolerate
along several axes through conits. The critical dirty fraction used here
is a conit-style numerical bound, and the tracking result of
Section~\ref{sec:x2-critical-sweep} is direct evidence that it behaves as
one in this setting.

\section{Multi-model consistency preservation}\label{sec:rw-multimodel}

Vitruvius~\cite{klare2021vitruvius} and the line of work on consistency
preservation rules provide the setting itself: automated propagation of
updates between models that represent the same system from different
viewpoints. That work treats propagation as a correctness mechanism to be
specified and executed, rather than as a source of transient inconsistency
to be measured and bounded.

\section{Simulating transactional multi-model systems}\label{sec:rw-simulators}

This thesis extends an existing simulator, and the work behind it is the
most directly relevant related work of all.

Transaction simulators have a long history in single-model settings. Ries
and Stonebraker~\cite{ries1979locking} used one to study locking
granularity under a workload held in equilibrium, and Dandamudi and
Au~\cite{dandamudi1991locking} extended that analysis to multiprocessor
database systems. Both establish the method this thesis relies on:
comparing concurrency-control policies under identical synthetic
workloads, in virtual time, so that the comparison is not confounded by
the host machine.

Zacouris and Acosta~\cite{zacouris2025m2ts} carry that method into
multi-model systems. Their simulator models a transactional server in
which an update to one model triggers consistency-preserving updates in
the models that overlap it, executed by bookkeeper transactions, and lets
the user vary concurrency, transaction size, semantic overlap and CPR
structure. The subsequent study~\cite{zacouris2026mxs} adds the second
execution semantics and the comparison this thesis starts from: under
ACID, the consistency-preserving work runs inside the triggering
transaction, and throughput degrades sharply as semantic overlap and CPR
density grow; under BASE, the work is deferred to background
reconciliation, and foreground throughput becomes largely insensitive to
both, at the price of transient inconsistency whose level depends on how
many processors are allocated to reconciliation.

Two results of that work are the direct starting point here. The first is
the throughput/consistency trade-off governed by reconciler provisioning:
too few reconcilers leave inconsistency high, too many starve foreground
execution. Section~\ref{sec:x1-validation} reproduces its shape and uses
it to validate the extended simulator. The second is the observation that
the best allocation depends on the configuration of the multi-model
system, which is precisely the property that makes reconciler provisioning
an awkward control: it is a knob whose correct setting must be found again
for every deployment.

What that work does not provide, and what this thesis adds, is a way to
select the execution semantics \emph{per transaction} and \emph{at
runtime}. In the base simulator, ACID and BASE are alternative
configurations of a whole run; here they coexist within one run, which
requires the arbiter of Section~\ref{sec:approach-arbiter} and makes the
tolerated inconsistency a configurable parameter rather than an outcome of
the provisioning.

Two rows carry the delimitation. Against the base simulator, the
difference is granularity and timing: semantics chosen per transaction at
admission rather than per run at configuration time. Against everything
above it, the difference is the last column, and it follows from the RQ3
result rather than from the literature. The existing runtime-adaptive
systems are steered by a proxy --- an expected cost, a staleness estimate,
a reconciler count --- so the consistency they achieve is emergent,
whereas $c$ is set in the units of the quantity it bounds.

Beyond the base simulator, all of the work above targets distributed
replicated storage under a single schema, where inconsistency means
divergence between copies of the same datum. None of it addresses
inconsistency generated by automatic consistency-preserving updates
between semantically overlapping models. That is the gap this thesis
fills.
