\chapter{Introduction}\label{chapter:introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Engineering systems maintain several models of the same product side by
side: CAD geometry, a simulation mesh, a written specification. These
models overlap semantically, in the sense that the same underlying fact is
represented in more than one of them. Keeping the representations in
agreement is automated through Consistency Preservation Rules (CPRs),
which take an update made in one model and propagate it into every model
that shares the affected information.

\plannedfigure{fig:motivating-example}{Motivating example: an update to
  one model propagates to two others through consistency preservation
  rules, either inside the transaction or after it commits.}{Three
  overlapping models --- CAD geometry, simulation mesh, specification ---
  with a write landing in one of them and the induced CPR work drawn twice:
  once on the critical path (ACID) and once deferred to a background
  reconciler (BASE), with the transiently inconsistent resources marked in
  the second case.}

The open question is \emph{when} that propagation is scheduled. Under ACID
semantics the CPR work sits on the critical path: a transaction does not
commit until every consequence of its write has been applied, so the
models are never observably inconsistent, and throughput pays for it.
Under BASE semantics the CPR work is handed to background reconcilers.
Foreground throughput is high, but the models are transiently
inconsistent, and how inconsistent they get is not something the operator
chooses.

Neither mode offers a way to state how much inconsistency is acceptable.
That is the gap this thesis addresses.

\section{Problem statement}\label{sec:intro-problem}

Under pure BASE, the inconsistency level is an emergent property of the
configuration. The only lever available is the number of reconciler
processors, $R$. It is coarse, indirect, and it costs foreground
throughput across the whole system. Under pure ACID, inconsistency is zero
by construction and throughput collapses. In neither case can an operator
ask for a given consistency level and have the system deliver it.

This thesis introduces a HYBRID execution mode, in which the choice
between ACID and BASE is made per transaction, at admission time, by a
component called a \emph{mode oracle}. The adaptive oracle makes that
choice from a threshold on the measured dirty fraction. The claim under
test is that this turns inconsistency from an emergent outcome into a
configurable target.

\section{Formal problem definition}\label{sec:intro-formal}

\subsection{System model}\label{sec:intro-formal-system}

A multi-model system is a finite set of resources $\mathcal{X}$ with
$|\mathcal{X}| = N$, partitioned across models $M_1, \dots, M_m$. Semantic
overlap is a relation on $\mathcal{X}$ that holds between resources
representing the same underlying fact. A consistency preservation rule
maps a write on a resource $x$ to the set
$\mathrm{cpr}(x) \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ of resources that must be updated
in consequence; applying it may trigger further rules, up to a bounded CPR
depth.

\subsection{Execution modes and inconsistency}\label{sec:intro-formal-modes}

A root transaction $t$ carries a mode
$\mu(t) \in \{\textsc{acid}, \textsc{base}\}$, fixed at admission and
inherited by every bookkeeper transaction it spawns. Under \textsc{acid},
the transitive CPR closure of $t$'s writes is applied inside $t$'s
boundary and $t$ commits only when that closure is complete. Under
\textsc{base}, $t$ commits after its own writes and the closure is handed
to background reconcilers.

The dirty set $D_k \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ is the set of resources carrying
a CPR update that has not yet been applied, and $d_k = |D_k| / N$ is the
dirty fraction. A run therefore has an inconsistency profile $\{d_k\}$
over simulated time rather than a single inconsistency value. It is
summarised by its 95th percentile $p_{95}(d)$: the level that the dirty
fraction stays below for at least 95\% of simulated time.

\subsection{Mode oracles and the resulting problem}\label{sec:intro-formal-problem}

A mode oracle is a function $\Omega$ that produces $\mu(t)$ for each
arriving root transaction from the state observable at admission. Pure
ACID and pure BASE are the two constant oracles. An oracle is
\emph{feedback-driven} if its output depends on the measured dirty
fraction and \emph{fixed} if it does not; the random oracle, which returns
\textsc{acid} with probability $p$, is the fixed oracle used as a
baseline.

Given a system instance, a reconciler provisioning $R$ and a target
inconsistency level $c$, the problem is to find an oracle $\Omega$ that
maximises throughput subject to

\begin{equation}
  p_{95}(d) \;\le\; c + \varepsilon
  \label{eq:problem}
\end{equation}

for a small tolerance $\varepsilon$, using the same setting of its control
parameter for every $R$. The two constant oracles solve degenerate
versions of this problem. Pure ACID satisfies Equation~\ref{eq:problem}
for every $c$ with $d \equiv 0$, at the lowest throughput available; pure
BASE maximises throughput and satisfies it only when $c$ happens to exceed
the level that the configuration produces on its own. The region between
them is unreachable without a per-transaction decision.

Allowing that decision creates a second, non-numerical problem. If two
transactions in different modes run concurrently, a resource can be locked
and dirty at the same time, a state that neither pure mode defines. The
problem above is therefore an optimisation problem and a well-definedness
problem at once, and the second must be solved first.

\section{Research questions}\label{sec:intro-rqs}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How can a multi-model transactional system integrate a
        \emph{hybrid mode} that safely adapts between ACID and BASE semantics
        at runtime, when its subsystems hold overlapping resources?

  \item[RQ2] How does the hybrid mode compare with pure ACID and pure BASE
        execution in throughput and inconsistency, and can the inconsistency it
        permits be configured?

  \item[RQ3] Does choosing the mode from the system's own internal metrics
        outperform a random ACID/BASE assignment on the same
        throughput/inconsistency trade-off?

\end{description}

In the terms of Section~\ref{sec:intro-formal}, RQ1 asks whether a
non-constant $\Omega$ can be made well defined at all, RQ2 asks what it
costs and whether $c$ controls the bound in Equation~\ref{eq:problem}, and
RQ3 asks whether $\Omega$ has to be feedback-driven or whether a fixed
oracle suffices.

\section{Overview of the approach}\label{sec:intro-approach-overview}

The approach has three parts. First, a HYBRID execution mode extends the
base simulator so that each root transaction is tagged \textsc{acid} or
\textsc{base} at admission and executes accordingly, with both paths
sharing the same resources, processors and CPR machinery. Second, because
the two paths now coexist, an arbiter defines what happens when they meet
on the same resource: ACID transactions wait for a dirty resource to be
reconciled, and BASE transactions yield on a locked one. This single rule
makes the mixed state well defined, and because the wait edge it adds
points one way only, it introduces no new deadlock. Third, the tagging
decision is delegated to a mode oracle. The adaptive oracle switches to
ACID when the smoothed dirty fraction reaches the configured threshold $c$
and back to BASE when it falls to a lower recovery threshold $r$; the
random oracle, which flips a coin with fixed probability $p$, is the
baseline that isolates what the feedback contributes.

The evaluation follows the same logic. Five parameter sweeps, 640
simulation runs in total, establish where the oracle engages, what the
mixture costs, whether $c$ controls the realised inconsistency, and
whether a fixed oracle can do the same job.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro-contributions}

\begin{description}
  \item[C1 (RQ1)] The HYBRID execution mode and the locked-XOR-dirty
        arbiter that makes it well defined, together with the argument that it
        admits no new deadlocks (Section~\ref{sec:approach-arbiter}).

  \item[C2 (RQ1)] Two mode oracles, an adaptive one that reads the system's
        own state and a random one used as a baseline, together with their
        control parameters (Section~\ref{sec:approach-oracles}).

  \item[C3 (RQ2)] A measurement of where the adaptive oracle engages, and a
        quantified explanation of what mixing the two modes costs: ACID
        transactions admitted during an ACID excursion block behind background
        reconciliation for a mean of up to 687 simulated seconds
        (Sections~\ref{sec:x1-reconciler-sweep} and~\ref{sec:x2-critical-sweep}).

  \item[C4 (RQ2)] Evidence that the critical threshold $c$ works as a
        configurable bound on the inconsistency tail: across $c$ from 2\% to
        30\% and $R$ from 1 to 15, the measured $p_{95}$ never differs
        from the configured $c$ by more than 0.74 percentage points
        (Section~\ref{sec:x2-critical-sweep}).

  \item[C5 (RQ3)] Evidence that this bound comes from the feedback loop and
        not from hybrid execution in general. Aiming at the same target with a
        fixed random split requires a control parameter that has to be
        recalibrated for every reconciler count, and whose realised effect varies
        by up to a factor of 44 across the range studied
        (Section~\ref{sec:x3-random-baseline}).
\end{description}

\section{Structure of the thesis}\label{sec:intro-structure}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:preliminaries} introduces the concepts the thesis
relies on: transactions and two-phase locking, BASE and reconciliation,
multi-model systems and CPRs, the base simulator this work extends, and
the two signal-handling ideas the oracle uses.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:related-work} positions the work against the
literature on combining ACID and BASE, on switching consistency at
runtime, and on bounded inconsistency.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:approach} describes the approach: the hybrid mode,
the correctness problem it creates, the arbiter that resolves it, and the
two oracles. Chapter~\ref{chapter:experiments} gives the experimental
settings and reports the four sweeps.
Chapter~\ref{chapter:conclusion} answers each research question, records
the lessons learned, and lists the open questions.
